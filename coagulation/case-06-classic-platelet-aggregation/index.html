The bleeding phenotype

Platelet disorders usually produce mucocutaneous bleeding: epistaxis, easy bruising, gingival bleeding, menorrhagia, and prolonged bleeding after procedures. Hemarthroses and deep-muscle hematomas more strongly suggest a coagulation-factor defect.

Inheritance clue

An affected sibling with clinically unaffected parents supports an autosomal recessive disorder.

What the aggregometer measures

PRP

Platelet-rich plasma

Relatively turbid and assigned approximately 0% aggregation. After an agonist is added, aggregation clears the suspension and light transmission rises.

PPP

Platelet-poor plasma

Relatively clear and used as the optical reference for approximately 100% light transmission.

Conceptual aggregation response↑ light transmission as aggregates form

Preanalytical requirements

Collect cleanly

Properly filled 3.2% sodium citrate tube; avoid traumatic draw, prolonged tourniquet use, and heparinized lines.

Handle gently

Keep at room temperature, do not refrigerate, and minimize agitation during transport.

Test promptly

Follow validated time limits and document medications, transfusions, platelet count, hematocrit, and bleeding history.

Count-matched control

At approximately 55 × 10⁹/L, the patient’s PRP is below the concentration at which conventional LTA is most robust. Compare with healthy control PRP diluted to a similar platelet count; do not normalize the patient PRP upward with PPP.

  1. Start with phenotype and smear. Lifelong mucocutaneous bleeding plus giant-platelet thrombocytopenia points toward an inherited macrothrombocytopenia.
  2. Respect optical limitations. Low platelet concentration and abnormal size can weaken LTA responses; use a count-matched control.
  3. Read the entire agonist pattern. Preserved ADP, epinephrine, collagen, arachidonic acid, and TRAP responses show intact activation and fibrinogen-mediated aggregation.
  4. Treat ristocetin differently. It interrogates plasma VWF binding to platelet GPIb-IX-V and produces agglutination.
  5. Use crossover studies to localize. Normal plasma cannot rescue patient platelets, while normal platelets respond in patient plasma.
  6. Confirm the receptor defect. Reduced CD42b/CD42a with preserved CD41/CD61 supports Bernard–Soulier syndrome.
  7. Complete the diagnosis. Biallelic pathogenic variants in GP1BA, GP1BB, or GP9 provide molecular confirmation.
One-sentence teaching summary
Bernard–Soulier syndrome produces giant-platelet thrombocytopenia with preserved conventional aggregation but absent ristocetin-induced agglutination because platelet GPIb-IX-V cannot bind VWF normally.
